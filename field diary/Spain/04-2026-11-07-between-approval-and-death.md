# 04. Between approval and death

*2026-11-07. Written the same day, from desk research.*

## What I did
Stepped back from the molecule to the state of assisted dying itself, five years after the LORE came into force. Read the Ministry's provisional 2025 data (presented in June 2026 for the fifth anniversary), the right-to-die association DMD's five-year report, press coverage of regional rates, and the two court cases that have shaped the law since 2024, both in Barcelona.

## What I found
- **Growing, but small.** 565 assisted deaths in 2025, a record: 0.13% of all deaths, one in every 750. 1,668 in total since 2021, from 3,716 requests (1,284 in 2025). Canada had 16,499 in 2025; the Netherlands 10,341.
- **Who.** Neurological diseases account for 46% of completed procedures. The Ministry reports 72% public support.
- **Dying while waiting.** One in three applicants dies before their request is resolved. A further 97 died after the commission had already approved them, with an average wait of 104.9 days in those cases.
- **Provisional numbers.** The full 2025 report is due at the end of 2026, and the Ministry says it will revise both the 2025 figures and the 2021 to 2025 totals.
- **Uneven geography.** Catalonia had the highest request rate in 2025: 6.14 per 100,000 inhabitants, almost three times the national average (2.61). Then Navarra (5.41) and the Basque Country (5.13). At the bottom, by euthanasia rate: Galicia, Castilla-La Mancha, Murcia, Extremadura.
- **Infrastructure catching up.** The draft 2026 Good Practice Manual recommends a dedicated euthanasia unit in every region, something DMD has asked for since 2021.
- **Noelia.** A 25-year-old Barcelona woman with paraplegia. Her euthanasia was suspended the day before it was due, on 1 August 2024, at her father's request, backed by the Catholic lawyers' association Abogados Cristianos. Catalan High Court in her favour (September 2025), Supreme Court (January 2026), Constitutional Court, and the European Court of Human Rights refusing to block it (10 March 2026). She died on 26 March 2026 in Sant Pere de Ribes, about a year and a half after the date first set.
- **Francesc.** A 55-year-old Barcelona man, suspended by his father's appeal. In May 2026 the Supreme Court ruled that people with a particularly close bond to the applicant can challenge an approval in court; a minority of judges warned this turns the right into a right to a bad death. His father, 96, then withdrew, and the procedure can go ahead.

## What I noticed
The commission's approval is not the end of the process. Between approval and death there is now a space where people wait, die before the date, or are taken to court. Since May 2026, a family member has a recognised legal path into that space. The court is a new actor in the chain, standing between the decision and the drug.

Both landmark cases happened in Barcelona. So did the deaths: Catalonia leads the requests, and Barcelona holds most of Catalonia's procedures (entry 03). The city is where the drug's lives concentrate and where the right to die is being contested.

And Navarra again. It sits second in requests, and first in vulture intoxications (entry 02). Both sites lead on both maps, human and animal.

The main thing: pentobarbital is almost absent from this picture. Most Spanish procedures are intravenous; the oral route is marginal (entry 01). In Spain, the drug that defines assisted dying elsewhere (Switzerland, the post I applied for) barely appears in it. Its absence may be the finding. Why does a country with a right to die choose not to hand people the glass?

## Open questions
- Does the oral route correlate with anything? Region, diagnosis, place of death (home, hospital, care home)?
- Does pentobarbital appear in any of the court cases, or only the procedure in general?
- What happens to a compounded oral dose when a death is suspended by a court the day before? Is it destroyed, stored, reported?
- How does the Swiss case look from here: a system built around the drug, against one built around the procedure?
