# 06. The slow afterlife

*2026-11-08. Written the same day, from desk research.*

## What I did
Followed the drug one step further than entry 02: past the carcass and the vulture, into soil, compost and water. Read the environmental science on pentobarbital's persistence, the US pet food cases, and the Spanish rules on where carcasses must go. Then asked where the drug is actually used in Spain, and found there is no public answer.

## What I found
**Persistence**
- **Chemistry.** Reference data notes that pentobarbital has no structure prone to hydrolysis and is not broken down by sunlight. Nothing in the molecule makes it fall apart on its own.
- **Groundwater.** Identified in a well 300 m from a landfill that took waste in 1968 and 1969; resampled in 1991, it was still there, at 1 mg/L.
- **Rivers.** In Germany, barbiturates were regularly detected in the Mulde, a tributary of the Elbe, at up to several µg/L. Groundwater affected by wastewater infiltration decades earlier still carried them. Lab tests showed no aerobic biodegradation and no hydrolysis.
- **Soil.** After 17 weeks in the lab, 17% remained in sand, 19% in topsoil, 10% in potting soil. Sterilised sand showed no degradation at all: what breaks it down, when anything does, is microbial life.
- **Compost.** Euthanised horses composted for 367 days: residues persisted with no clear downward trend, and soil below the pile showed the drug had leached through the pad. In a burial pit, xylazine degraded quickly; pentobarbital stayed in the leachate for weeks.
- **Feed.** In the US, pentobarbital reached pet food in 2017 and 2018. The FDA found no dog or cat DNA and assumed it came from euthanised, rendered cattle or horses, via tallow. A rendering supplier kept selling contaminated tallow after being alerted.
- **Spain.** I found no Spanish study of pentobarbital in soil or water. Strong wildlife toxicology on vultures (entry 02), apparently no environmental monitoring of the drug itself. A gap, and possibly a contribution.

**Where it is used**
No public data on pentobarbital use by region or sector that I know of. The map has to be built from proxies and requests:
- Companion animal clinics and shelters, probably the largest use, now shaped by Law 7/2023 (entry 05).
- Equine and livestock on farms, especially extensive cattle in the north.
- Laboratory research: rodents are routinely injected with pentobarbital; the Ministry of Agriculture publishes annual figures on animals used in research.
- Zoos and wildlife recovery centres.
- Production, in the Vallès corridor (entry 03).

**Where the carcass must go**
- Pets are Category 1 animal by-products, the highest-risk class: incineration by authorised companies, burial in authorised pet cemeteries, or authorised educational or research use.
- Burial varies by region. Galicia has allowed burying pets on private property since 2016. Asturias bans burial on pastures except in remote areas where collection is impossible. Madrid has authorised pet burial where there is no public health risk. Extremadura's 2024 rules require the burial site's coordinates.
- Pet cemeteries are a growing sector; the Ministry lists over 150 authorised incineration plants for animal by-products.
- Livestock normally goes to collection and rendering or incineration, with exceptions for scavenger feeding zones (entry 05) and burial in inaccessible areas.
- Every leaflet: dispose of under national law, never feed to other animals.

## What I noticed
This is where MORTALMED meets my own work.

**Specimenography.** Its premise is residues that persist outside the networks that produced them. Pentobarbital is exactly that: a molecule made to end one life, still active in a carcass, a compost pile, a well, decades later, long after the clinic, the farmer and the paperwork have moved on. And the instrument that found it in burial leachate, GC-MS, is one of the instruments I already treat as an epistemological partner. Reciprocal capture, in practice: the lab makes the residue visible, the ethnography makes it mean something.

**Nitrates.** In *Toxic Afterlives* and *The Flush* I follow nitrate from industrial farming as a residue that travels: diffuse, massive, carried by water into aquifers. Pentobarbital is its opposite and its companion. Nitrate is a residue of production, spread across whole landscapes; pentobarbital is a residue of care, concentrated in single bodies, tiny in quantity, potent, pharmaceutical. Both come out of the livestock economy, both move through soil and water, both outlive the decisions that released them. Together they give two scales of the same afterlife: the aquifer and the carcass.

**The geography of disposal is the geography of persistence.** Every carcass destination is a possible site where the drug might stay: feeding zones, pet cemeteries, private burials in Galicia, inaccessible-area burials in Asturias, old landfills. The protocols do not say where the drug goes; they say where the body goes. The drug follows.

## A note for the plan (entry 05)
- Persistence fits Year 2 (rural) and Year 4 (specimens), not as a measuring campaign of my own but as a collaboration. It needs an environmental chemistry partner, permissions and a lab.
- First step: ask the IREC toxicologists whether anyone in Spain has looked for pentobarbital in soil or water.
- Possible specimens: soil cores from beneath a feeding zone or an authorised pet cemetery, paired with their lab results. The cabinet as the place where the slow afterlife becomes visible.
- Keep the scale honest: nitrate is everywhere; pentobarbital is rare. The argument is about persistence and care, not quantity.

## Open questions
- Does pentobarbital reach Spanish groundwater at all, or is the risk only local, beneath carcasses?
- Which carcass destinations leave the drug in the ground (burial, feeding zones) and which destroy it (incineration)?
- What happens to pentobarbital in rendering in Spain? Are euthanised animals excluded from feed chains, and how is that checked?
- Could a pet cemetery be a site? Mourning, care and residue in the same patch of soil.
