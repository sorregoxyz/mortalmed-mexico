# 04. From request to release

*2026-11-08. Written the same day, from desk research.*

## What I did
The job ad says the research will follow people through assessment and preparation, and how a planned death becomes authorised. The diary did not have that procedure yet. Pieced it together from how EXIT and Dignitas describe their own process (press interviews and secondary sources), the SAMW guidelines, a legal commentary on how the drug is taken, and, most usefully, the Basel-Landschaft government's answers to parliamentary questions on what police do after an assisted suicide.

## What I found: the procedure, step by step

**1. Membership.** Organisations accompany members only. EXIT charges CHF 45 a year or CHF 1,100 for life. Dignitas also starts with membership, then a formal application.

**2. Documents.** EXIT requires a doctor's certificate of capacity, a diagnosis and a prescription. If the person's own doctor refuses, EXIT can refer them to a doctor it works with. Dignitas sends the medical file to one of its physicians, who decides whether they would be ready to prescribe.

**3. The provisional green light (Dignitas).** If the physician says yes in principle, the member gets a "provisional green light". Ludwig Minelli, Dignitas's founder, claimed that 70% of those who get it never call again: the green light works as an emergency exit that calms them.

**4. Consultations.** The SAMW guidelines (2022) ask for at least two detailed discussions with the doctor, at least two weeks apart, except in justified exceptions. Organisations fought this, arguing it is especially hard for people travelling from abroad. Dignitas describes two consultations with an independent doctor, separated in time.

**5. The funnel.** EXIT's own figures for 2017 show the shape: about 3,500 requests, 1,031 assessments, 734 people who received pentobarbital. Most requests never reach the drug.

**6. The prescription and the pharmacy.** A doctor writes the prescription; the drug is collected from a pharmacy, sometimes by a member or employee of the organisation, which requires a written authorisation from the person. Cantonal pharmacists have issued a position paper on how dispensing should run. Swissmedic has called it "delicate" when one person holds several roles in a case (entry 03, Preisig).

**7. The act.** At the agreed time, the companion (Freitodbegleiter) brings the drug. Families are almost always present. The person must take the last step: drinking the solution dissolved in water, injecting it through a stomach tube, or opening an intravenous line themselves. An antiemetic is usually given first to prevent vomiting. Sleep follows within minutes. EXIT says it uses pentobarbital exclusively. Dignitas has had a house since 2009 where foreigners die.

**8. After death: an "extraordinary death".** Every assisted suicide is an *aussergewöhnlicher Todesfall* under Article 253 of the Criminal Procedure Code. A doctor must confirm death; police, and in some cantons the prosecutor, must be called. In Zurich, one person each from the prosecutor's office, the police and forensic medicine attend.

**9. What the police check (Basel-Landschaft).** The canton's government listed it in an answer to a parliamentary question:
- the dead person's identity;
- the signed declaration of the wish to die (original, or on video);
- a copy of the pentobarbital prescription, not older than six months;
- the authorisation, if the drug was collected by the organisation;
- the protocol of the death, including an infusion report;
- that the person took the drug themselves, or was able to open the infusion themselves;
- that the prescription and the drug's container match.
The Institute of Forensic Medicine in Basel is notified and may come to the scene; the prosecutor on call is informed.

**10. Release.** Once the authorities are satisfied, the body is released and everything continues as after any other death. In Basel-Landschaft, such cases usually end with a decision not to open proceedings. A neighbouring canton, Solothurn, has an agreement with an organisation covering flat-rate costs of these investigations; Basel-Landschaft's parliament has discussed it.

## What I noticed
**The drug's paperwork ends with the police.** The last thing anyone does with pentobarbital in Switzerland is check the empty container against the prescription. The drug starts as a document and ends as evidence. Between the two, its whole life is paper: certificate, prescription, authorisation, declaration, protocol, report. A specimen in the most literal sense, assembled by the state after the fact.

**Spain reviews before, Switzerland after.** In Spain a commission authorises the death in advance (Spain entry 04); in Switzerland nobody authorises it, and every death is investigated afterwards as a possible crime, then closed. One system is a permission; the other is an inquest. That may be the cleanest contrast between the two diaries.

**The green light as a drug in itself.** If 70% never come back, the prescription works for most people not as a means but as a possibility. The provisional green light is a pharmacological object with no molecule in it: it calms. That fits *pharmakon* uncomfortably well.

**The funnel is where most of the ethnography would be.** 3,500 requests, 734 deaths. The question for fieldwork is less "who dies" than what happens to everyone in between.

## Open questions
- Who are the "doctors EXIT works with", and how many prescriptions does each write?
- What does the cantonal pharmacists' position paper say, and which pharmacies in the Basel area dispense?
- What happens to a dispensed dose that is never used (the person changes their mind, or dies first)?
- How long does a typical case take from first contact to release, and where does it stall?
- What does the forensic institute actually examine on site, and how often does it come?
